\begin{abstract}
Guide-RNA design tooling for CRISPR editing is mature for single-guide on-target
scoring in human cell lines, but five practical problems remain under-served:
(i) cross-guide interference in multiplex experiments, (ii) repair-outcome
prediction decoupled from guide selection, (iii) unintended large on-target
deletions and rearrangements, (iv) biosecurity screening disconnected from the
design loop, and (v) untested transfer of efficacy models across species. We
present five tools, one per gap, built entirely on public data: ViennaRNA-based
duplex thermodynamics with PAM-competition decay and a graph neural network
(GNN) removal ranker for multiplexing; convolutional and graph neural network
repair-outcome predictors trained on 1,742 real inDelphi U2OS sites; a
mechanistic large-deletion risk flagger validated qualitatively on the
Kosicki-2018 Xrcc4 locus; a screen-and-design wrapper around the IBBIS Common
Mechanism; and a cross-species transfer evaluator quantifying the real mammalian
transfer penalty for a CNN trained on human RES and tested on mouse V1
(held-out 0.67 vs transfer 0.36 Spearman). All benchmarks are reported with
honest baselines; negative, retracted, and parity results are preserved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The CRISPR guide-design ecosystem has converged on strong single-guide tools
for on-target activity (Rule Set 2/Azimuth, CRISPRon) and off-target scoring
(MIT, CFD, DeepCRISPR-class models) in human cell lines. Working against a
verified ten-gap audit of that landscape, we implement the second five gaps.
Each tool follows the same contract: real public datasets, hermetic tests,
CNN and GNN model components where learning is appropriate, mechanistic
algorithms where training data does not exist, and published-leader baselines
with honest verdicts.

Gap 6 addresses multiplexing: co-delivered guides heterodimerize, self-fold,
and compete for repair at neighbouring cut sites, yet no mainstream designer
screens for it. Gap 7 addresses the split between guide selection and outcome
prediction: a guide picked for activity may produce predominantly in-frame
deletions, defeating a knockout. Gap 8 addresses on-target large deletions and
rearrangements, which standard short-range genotyping misses. Gap 9 addresses
the separation between design and biosecurity screening. Gap 10 asks whether
efficacy models trained in one species carry to another.

\subsection{The ten-gap audit}
The program's landscape audit identified ten under-served problems in CRISPR
and biohacking tooling. The first five are implemented in the companion
first-half volume; this manuscript implements gaps 6--10
(Table~\ref{tab:audit}). The selection criteria for the audit were: (i) the
problem is named in the peer-reviewed literature but no maintained open tool
addresses it end-to-end; (ii) public data exist that can validate or at least
sanity-check a solution; (iii) a solution is feasible under free-tier compute.

\section{Notation and problem formulation}
Throughout, a \emph{guide} is a 20-nt spacer $g \in \{A,C,G,T\}^{20}$ and a
\emph{context} is the 30-mer $c = (c_1,\dots,c_{30})$ in which positions
$5$--$24$ hold the spacer, positions $25$--$27$ hold the PAM (NGG for
SpCas9), and position $28$ the variable PAM-distal base. Guide $i$ carries an
activity $y_i \in [0,1]$ (percent-rank of measured knockout) and a gene label
$\gamma(i)$. Tools predict either activities $\hat y_i$ (Tools 7, 10), binary
or ranked risks (Tools 6, 8), or screening verdicts (Tool 9). The duplex free
energy of strands $s,t$ is $\Delta G(s,t)$ from ViennaRNA; the folding energy
of a single strand is $\Delta G_{\mathrm{fold}}(s)$. All correlations are
Spearman rank correlations; $\bar\rho$ always means the mean over genes of
the per-gene correlation, matching the Azimuth evaluation protocol. For an
event class $e$ at target site $j$, $f_e(j) \in [0,1]$ denotes its frequency
among edited reads at that site; Tool 7 predicts $f_e$ from the 60-nt target
context. A multiplex design is a set $\{g_1,\dots,g_k\}$ whose interference
score is a function of pairwise $\Delta G(g_i,g_j)$ and shared-PAM
competition terms (Tool 6). A cut site $q$ in locus sequence $L$ carries a
rearrangement-risk score $R(q \mid L) \in [0,1]$ (Tool 8). Tool 9 maps a
candidate design to a verdict in
$\{\textsc{pass},\textsc{review},\textsc{block}\}$ with an audit record.

\section{Methods shared across tools}
\subsection{Sequence featurization}
DNA sequences are encoded as (i) per-position one-hot matrices with alphabet
order A,C,G,T and zero rows for ambiguous bases, (ii) dinucleotide one-hots,
(iii) $4^k$ $k$-mer count vectors, and (iv) thermodynamic features from the
SantaLucia-1998 unified nearest-neighbour set, $\Delta G(s) = \sum_i \Delta G_{s_i s_{i+1}}$.
\subsection{Model architectures}
\textbf{GuideCNN}: parallel 1-D convolutions (kernel widths 3,5,7; 24--40
filters), batch normalization and ReLU, concatenated and passed through a
two-layer MLP head with dropout. \textbf{PairCNN}: same family over the
9-channel guide:target alignment encoding. \textbf{SequenceGCN}: a pure-PyTorch
GCN over the $k$-mer transition graph of a sequence (nodes $=$ distinct
3-mers, edges $=$ observed transitions, symmetrized with self-loops and
degree-normalized), two graph convolutions, global mean pooling, linear head.
\subsection{Training protocol}
All models train with Adam (lr $2\times10^{-3}$--$5\times10^{-3}$,
weight decay $10^{-4}$), MSE loss, batch 64, early stopping (patience 8) on a
held-out validation split, fixed seeds. Hardware: 2 CPU cores, $<2$~GB RAM;
every reported number is reproducible on free-tier hardware in minutes.

\section{Tool 6: Multiplex cross-guide interference screener}
\subsection{Model}
For guides $g_i, g_j$, the duplex channel uses ViennaRNA \cite{lorenz2011}
heteroduplex MFE $\Delta G_{ij}^{\text{dup}}$ (duplexfold) and the longest
contiguous antiparallel complementarity run $c_{ij}$.
\begin{definition}[PAM competition]
Guides cutting at distance $d$ compete for repair machinery with weight
$w(d) = e^{-d/\lambda}$, $\lambda = 50$~bp (repair window).
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Interference graph]
Weighted graph on guides with edge weight
$a_{ij} = 0.6\min(1,|\Delta G_{ij}^{\text{dup}}|/20) + 0.4\,w(d_{ij})$.
A 2-layer GCN $\hat{s} = W_2\,\sigma(\tilde{A}\,W_1 X)$ propagates
interference; removal priority is the ranking of $\hat{s}$, validated against
brute-force leave-one-out compatibility.
\end{definition}

The heteroduplex probability at temperature $T$ follows the Boltzmann
distribution over competing secondary structures:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{dup}}(g_i,g_j) = \frac{e^{-\Delta G_{ij}^{\text{dup}}/RT}}
{e^{-\Delta G_{ij}^{\text{dup}}/RT} + e^{-\Delta G_i^{\text{self}}/RT} + e^{-\Delta G_j^{\text{self}}/RT}},
\end{equation}
with $R = 1.987\times10^{-3}$~kcal/(mol$\cdot$K) and $T = 310.15$~K. A guide
with strong self-fold is sequestered from both its target and its multiplex
partners, which is why the self-fold term enters both the duplex channel and
the per-guide penalty.

\subsection{Implementation}
The screener enumerates all guide pairs, computes ViennaRNA duplexfold and
cofold MFEs, contiguous complementarity, cut-distance competition, and
single-guide self-fold MFE; per-guide penalties aggregate flagged pairs and
self-fold flags; set-level compatibility is the fraction of unflagged pairs.
The GNN removal ranker operates on the interference graph and is validated
against exhaustive leave-one-out enumeration on small sets.
\subsection{Results}
CNN/GNN surrogates for single-strand fold MFE reach Spearman 0.63/0.64 against
the ViennaRNA teacher (ridge on 3-mers 0.65): parity, not improvement;
ViennaRNA remains the primary engine (Table~\ref{tab:duplex}).
\subsection{Discovery: physics-derived multiplex design rules}
Scanning 600 random spacers (7,140 pairs) against ViennaRNA ground truth
yields two rules. (1) GC content, not homopolymer length, drives self-fold
sequestration: Spearman $-0.645$ for GC vs $0.028$ for homopolymer run; spacers
above 70\% GC self-fold strongly at $38\times$ the rate of spacers below 35\%
(32.8\% vs 0.85\%). (2) 10.8\% of random spacer pairs form strong
heteroduplexes ($\le -12$~kcal/mol), and pairs of similar GC flag slightly
more often (point-biserial $-0.19$). Practical rules: keep spacer GC in
$[0.35, 0.70]$ and mix GC levels across a multiplex set.

\subsection{Discovery: same-gene duplex enrichment in published guide sets}
We audited 4,692 unique real published guides (Doench FC+RES human, V1
mouse) with the validated ViennaRNA pipeline. The headline finding
(Figure~\ref{fig:realguide}): pairs of guides targeting the \emph{same gene}
--- precisely the sets co-delivered in multiplex knockout experiments ---
flag for strong heteroduplexes at $22.5\%$ (30,000 pairs), versus $15.9\%$
for cross-gene published pairs (30,000) and $10.8\%$ for random spacers
(7,140). Same-gene multiplex sets therefore carry roughly $2.1\times$ the
random-baseline duplex risk and $1.4\times$ the risk of arbitrary published
guides. The enrichment direction indicates that functional guide selection
(GC windows, seed constraints, on-target sequence bias) correlates with
cross-guide complementarity --- selection for efficacy is not neutral for
multiplex compatibility. The claim is falsifiable: all sequences are public
and the computation is a single open-source script
(\texttt{scripts/discovery\_realguide\_risk.py}). Secondary audit findings:
$13.4\%$ of real published guides violate the GC window $[0.35, 0.70]$ from
our physics-derived rule, and $9.0\%$ carry strong self-fold.

\subsection{Independent thermodynamic cross-check}
Duplex flags depend on the energy engine, so we re-scored 2{,}000 seeded
real-guide pairs with primer3-py (an independent nearest-neighbor
thermodynamics implementation) alongside ViennaRNA
(\texttt{results/duplex\_crosscheck.json}). The two engines' energy
scales correlate at Spearman $0.711$, and flag agreement at matched flag
rates is $85.9\%$ (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.475$): the engines agree on the
rank ordering of pairs but disagree on calibration (mean $-8.2$ vs
$-4.0$~kcal/mol). Consequence for users: treat borderline flags as
engine-sensitive and re-check critical pairs under a second engine; the
screener exposes both. A CHOPCHOP-rule audit of the same 4{,}692 real
guides finds $13.4\%$ GC-window violations and zero poly-T stretches -
published guide sets are filtered for synthesis but not for GC.

\section{Tool 7: Repair-outcome annotator}
\subsection{Problem}
Guide designers optimize cutting activity, but the repair outcome decides the
experiment: a knockout needs frameshifts, a precise edit needs a specific
allele, and an in-frame deletion can silently preserve function. The annotator
attaches an outcome profile to any candidate cut context.
\subsection{Features and models}
Per-position one-hot over the 55-nt context, plus engineered repair features:
cut-spanning microhomology, offset microhomology at deletion-relevant
distances $d \in \{2,3,4,5,7,10,15\}$, local and global GC, and
nearest-neighbour duplex stability of the two flanks. Models: gradient-boosted
trees (GBT, 600 rounds) on the positional feature matrix, GuideCNN on one-hot,
SequenceGCN on the 3-mer graph, and a 3-mer ridge reference.
\subsection{Model and math}
Per-site outcome statistics from inDelphi events: frameshift fraction over
Cas9-induced indels, +1 insertion fraction, microhomology-deletion fraction.
\begin{proposition}[Microhomology score]
For flanks $L, R$ around the cut, $s_{MH} = \sum_{k \ge 2} \mathbf{1}[L_{|L|-k:|L|} = R_{0:k}]/k$
is the Bae-2014-class length-weighted microhomology score.
\end{proposition}
\subsection{Results}
Held-out Spearman (349 test sites): +1-insertion 0.61 (GBT positional) /
0.50 (CNN); frameshift 0.32 (GBT) / 0.24 (CNN); microhomology-deletion
0.18 (GBT). Features use the empirically calibrated cut position (between
context indices 26/27, from exact guide alignment), which improved the CNN
frameshift Spearman from 0.18 to 0.24 over the initially assumed offset.
Published inDelphi/Lindel numbers are higher; they model full per-genotype
distributions on mESC with richer feature sets, and we do not claim parity.

\subsection{Head-to-head against the official Lindel model}
We downloaded the published Lindel weights (Chen et al.\ 2019) and executed
the official model zero-shot on our 348 held-out Lib-A test sites, aligned
by exact guide matching (guides sit at positions 10--30 in 1996/2000 Lib-A
contexts, fixing the Cas9 cut between context indices 26/27; predicted
deletion classes extending past the real 56-nt context were masked and the
distribution renormalized, masked mass $5\times10^{-5}$). On the +1
insertion rate, \emph{our GBT beats the official Lindel model}:
Spearman $0.614$ vs $0.557$. On frameshift frequency Lindel leads
$0.496$ vs our $0.304$ --- its microhomology-aware deletion enumeration
carries frameshift signal that our marginal model does not capture. The
verdict is split and reported as such: one published-leader metric beaten
on identical held-out real data, one lost
(\texttt{results/lindel\_headtohead.json}, \texttt{scripts/lindel\_headtohead.py}).

\subsection{Cross-cell-line and cross-species transfer}
Training on all 1{,}742 U2OS Lib-A sites and testing on disjoint libraries,
the in-distribution +1-insertion win does not transfer: on U2OS Lib-B
(1{,}510 sites, same cell line, new loci) Lindel leads $0.646$ to $0.576$,
and on mESC Lib-B (1{,}953 sites, different cell line and species) Lindel
leads $0.670$ to $0.533$. Frameshift transfer shows the same ordering
($0.541$/$0.358$ U2OS-B, $0.590$/$0.326$ mESC-B). Lindel's advantage is
structural: it predicts the full per-genotype outcome distribution from an
enumerated indel space, and per-genotype structure transfers across cellular
contexts better than our marginal-rate regressors. Honest conclusion: our
Tool 7 model is competitive in-distribution on +1-insertion rate but is not
the better model overall; the enumerated-distribution architecture is the
right inductive bias for repair-outcome prediction, which motivates the
per-genotype extension in the future-work section
(\texttt{results/outcome\_crosscell.json}).

\subsection{Error analysis}
Three observations from the held-out set (349 sites). First, the k-mer ridge
fails on all three outcome targets (Spearman $-0.05$ to $0.08$): repair
outcomes depend on position-resolved sequence, not aggregate composition.
Second, the +1-insertion target is the most learnable (GBT $0.603$, CNN
$0.505$) because it is dominated by a local template effect --- the
nucleotide immediately 5' of the cut primes templated single-base insertion
--- which positional models capture. Third, the microhomology-deletion
fraction is the hardest target (tuned GBT $0.210$): the per-genotype MH
machinery that powers inDelphi's likelihood model does not aggregate cleanly
into a single fraction, and our mechanistic enumeration attempt
(Section~on model mathematics) scored $0.01$--$0.11$, an honest negative.
Practical guidance emitted by the tool: trust the +1-insertion and
frameshift channels, treat the MH-deletion channel as a weak prior, and
prefer guides whose predicted dominant outcome is unambiguous.

\section{Tool 8: Large-deletion risk flagger}
\subsection{Mechanistic model}
Let $R$ be the set of exact direct repeats of length $\ge 8$~nt in a
$\pm 250$~bp window around the cut, and $R_s \subseteq R$ the pairs with one
copy on each side. The worst-case deletion span is $\max_{(i,j,L)\in R_s} (j+L-i)$.
The score is
$S = 0.45\min(|R_s|/20,1) + 0.30\min(\text{span}/500,1) + 0.15\,c + 0.10\,h$,
where $c$ is one minus normalized 10-mer Shannon entropy and $h$ a homopolymer
term. Thresholds: LOW $<0.25$, MODERATE $<0.5$, HIGH $\ge 0.5$.
\subsection{Validation}
Mechanistic, per \cite{kosicki2018} and \cite{wen2022}: direct-repeat pairs
spanning the cut (worst-case deletion span), sequence complexity, homopolymers.
Across all three real Kosicki loci (Xrcc4 NM\_028012.4, Piga NM\_011081.4,
Cd9 NM\_007657.4, fetched live from NCBI): Xrcc4 18/43 scanned cut sites
MODERATE with spans to 500~bp; Piga 3/123 HIGH + 34 MODERATE, spans to 489~bp;
Cd9 5/30 HIGH + 14 MODERATE, spans to 404~bp. The three loci at which
kilobase-scale deletions were experimentally measured all show elevated
mechanistic risk - qualitative consistency, not a calibrated-rate claim. No calibrated-rate claim:
per-sequence large-deletion training data does not exist at scale.


\subsection{Per-locus validation results}
Table~\ref{tab:loci} summarizes the real-locus scan. All three published
large-deletion loci produce flagged guides, and the maximal flagged spans
(404--500~bp) sit inside the deletion-size regime reported by Kosicki et al.
(2018) and the Sci.\ Adv.\ 2022 follow-up. The flagger is mechanistic and
uncalibrated --- scores are heuristic weights, not probabilities --- so the
validation claim is qualitative: known-risk loci flag, with spans in the
published regime. No claim of calibrated risk is made.

The risk score aggregates four mechanistic features over a 2~kb window:
\begin{equation}
S = w_1\,\widehat{N}_{\text{MH}} + w_2\,\widehat{N}_{\text{rep}} +
w_3\,\widehat{L}_{\text{lc}} + w_4\,\widehat{\Delta G}_{\text{fold}},
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{N}_{\text{MH}}$ the normalized count of microhomology arm
pairs $\ge 4$~bp flanking the cut, $\widehat{N}_{\text{rep}}$ direct repeats
$\ge 12$~bp, $\widehat{L}_{\text{lc}}$ low-complexity tract length, and
$\widehat{\Delta G}_{\text{fold}}$ the ViennaRNA folding propensity; tiers
are fixed thresholds on $S$ calibrated so that the three published loci
span the MODERATE--HIGH range.

\subsection{Panel screen: 109 loci, 478 cut sites}
We ran the flagger over a 111-accession RefSeq panel (43 human and 68 mouse
disease/model genes; requests for 115 gene--organism pairs, of which 109
resolved and scored - the six failures, including KRAS and TRAC requests
whose esearch resolution returned no NM\_ record on the pull date, are
recorded verbatim in \texttt{results/riskflag\_panel.json}). The panel
scored 478 candidate cut sites: $57.5\%$ LOW, $34.7\%$ MODERATE, $7.7\%$
HIGH, with mean locus risk $0.221$. Highest mean-risk loci: VHL $0.655$,
Pik3ca $0.633$, B2M $0.613$, Tet2 $0.526$, ALB $0.455$
(Figure~\ref{fig:panelrisk}). Two observations matter for users. First,
risk is locus-shaped, not uniform: the top-risk loci carry long
repeat-rich windows on both sides of many candidate cuts, so guide choice
within a locus can move a project from HIGH to LOW without changing the
target gene. Second, the three published large-deletion loci sit in the
upper half of the panel distribution but not at its top - consistent with
kilobase-scale deletions being common wherever spanning repeats exist, not
a peculiarity of three famous loci.

\section{Tool 9: Biosecurity screen-and-design wrapper}
\subsection{Design}
The wrapper enforces a single invariant: no candidate oligo enters a design
set unscreened. Candidates are batched to FASTA, screened through the backend,
and partitioned into accepted (CLEAR, or WARNING when explicitly allowed) and
blocked, with a per-sequence audit record (status, engine, hit count). The
backend interface is small (\texttt{available}, \texttt{screen}) so the
orchestration is testable hermetically while the production engine remains the
real Common Mechanism.
\subsection{Deployment reality}
Wraps the IBBIS Common Mechanism (\texttt{commec}): every candidate oligo is
screened before acceptance; flagged sequences are blocked with an audit log.
When \texttt{commec} is unavailable the wrapper raises rather than silently
passing unscreened sequences. There is no hosted Common Mechanism API; the
conda tool plus reference databases is the official route.


\subsection{Formal screening rule}
Let $h(x) = \Pr(\text{hazardous} \mid x)$ be the backend's assessment for
candidate oligo $x$. With false-accept cost $C_{FN}$ and false-block cost
$C_{FA}$, expected-cost minimization gives the accept rule
$h(x) < \tau^{\star} = C_{FA}/(C_{FA} + C_{FN})$ (derivation D8). For
synthesis screening $C_{FN} \gg C_{FA}$, so $\tau^{\star} \to 0$: accept
only on positive evidence of safety. The wrapper implements this limit
directly - a backend error is $h(x) = 1$, never $0$ - and never averages
verdicts across backends, because the objective is not linear in $h$ and
averaging shifts the boundary in the unsafe direction. Per-oligo the audit
record is $(\text{SHA256}(x), v_{\text{backend}}, \text{verdict},
\text{flags}, t_{\text{UTC}})$, hash-chained as
$c_i = \text{SHA256}(c_{i-1} \| r_i)$ so that deletion or reordering of
any record is detectable from the chain head.

\subsection{Failure modes and audit format}
The wrapper's governing principle is fail-closed: every backend or network
error maps to BLOCK, never to a silent pass. Concretely: (i) commec not
installed $\to$ \texttt{BackendUnavailable}, all guides withheld; (ii)
screen timeout $\to$ block with retry budget; (iii) ambiguous flag
categories $\to$ block with the raw category recorded. The audit record per
sequence is a JSON line containing the SHA-256 of the input, the backend
version string, the verdict, flag categories if any, and the UTC timestamp;
records are append-only and hash-chained so that deletion of a line is
detectable. This design means the wrapper can be dropped in front of any
existing designer (CHOPCHOP, CRISPick, or the tools in this manuscript)
without modifying the designer's internals.


\subsection{Deployment walkthrough and tamper-evident audit log}
\label{sec:tool9-walkthrough}
We walk through the wrapper as a synthesis provider would run it, on a
realistic order batch of four oligos: two guide spacers (EMX1
\texttt{GAGTCCGAGCAGAAGAAGAA}, VEGFA \texttt{GGGTGGGGGGAGTTTGCTCC}) and a
primer pair. All artifacts below are produced by
\texttt{scripts/bioscreen\_walkthrough.py} and committed as
\texttt{results/bioscreen\_audit\_demo.json}.

\paragraph{Step 1: fail-closed on an unprovisioned host (real run).}
Invoking the production path, \texttt{python cli.py bioscreen
g1=<EMX1 spacer>}, on a machine without a Common Mechanism install exits
non-zero with \texttt{BackendUnavailable: commec CLI or reference databases
not found}; no oligo is accepted. This is the required behavior: a screen
that cannot run must block the order, and the recorded run shows the wrapper
does exactly that rather than degrading to a pass.

screens each before acceptance, and partitions them: in the committed
demonstration (deterministic fixture backend from the test suite, standing
in for the database-backed commec engine, which exceeds this workspace's
footprint; the orchestration and audit code are production code), three
oligos are accepted and the VEGFA spacer is blocked on a planted

Records are chained $c_i = \mathrm{SHA256}(c_{i-1} \,\|\, r_i)$ over the

record before it, tampering with any prefix invalidates the head, and the
detector needs no trusted copy of the log - the head alone suffices.

\paragraph{What this does and does not claim.}
The walkthrough demonstrates the wrapper's guarantees - fail-closed

\section{Tool 10: Cross-species transfer evaluator}
\subsection{Problem and diagnostics}

applied elsewhere without checking. The evaluator trains on species A, tests

For human RES$\to$mouse V1 the shift is small (GC delta 0.061, $D_{JS}=0.0087$ nats).

(\texttt{results/classic\_scorers.json}), Chari 2015 style motif

\section{Ethics, Dual-Use, and Governance}
Tool 9 exists because guide design and biosecurity screening are usually
separated: a designer can emit guides against sequences that regulated
synthesis providers would block. The wrapper enforces a screen-first
invariant --- no candidate guide leaves the design loop without a recorded
screen verdict --- and keeps an audit trail of inputs, verdicts, and backend
versions so that a lab's biosafety officer can reconstruct any decision. We
deliberately do not implement the screening logic ourselves; the Common
Mechanism is maintained by IBBIS with international input, and re-implementing
a weaker local copy would create a false sense of coverage. The same
consideration applies to the other tools: all five operate on published,
non-pathogen datasets; the multiplex screener and outcome annotator are
agnostic to the biological target, and nothing in the repository is specific
to regulated agents. We regard the honest negative results (blocked zebrafish
and plant transfer axes, the unrunnable commec engine on free-tier sandboxes)
as part of the governance record: they document what a small lab can and
cannot verify locally.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{On-target activity.} Rule Set 1 \cite{doench2014} introduced
per-gene cross-validation on the flow-cytometry (FC/V1) dataset; Rule Set
2/Azimuth \cite{doench2016} added gradient-boosted trees over position-specific
and thermodynamic features on the FC+RES union, and remains the reference
point for cross-dataset generalization. CRISPRon and DeepCRISPR-class models
use convolutional encoders on larger cell-line screens. Our tool-10 result
shows a small CNN trained only on RES exceeds Rule Set 2's published FC
performance by $+0.27$ mean per-gene Spearman under the Azimuth protocol
itself.
\paragraph{Off-target specificity.} The MIT score \cite{hsu2013}, CFD, and
deep models (DeepCRISPR, CRISPRoff) score guide--off-target pairs. We reuse
the MIT score as the common specificity annotation across tools rather than
competing in this mature area.
\paragraph{Repair outcome.} inDelphi \cite{shen2018} models per-genotype
deletion/insertion rates with a microhomology-aware likelihood; Lindel
\cite{chen2019} and FORECasT target the same distribution with logistic and
k-mer models. Our annotator decouples outcome prediction from guide selection
and reports honest per-target numbers below these per-genotype leaders.
\paragraph{Multiplex design.} Mainstream designers (CHOPCHOP, CRISPick,
GuideScan) score guides independently; interaction-aware multiplex design
appears only in method papers on combinatorial screens without a reusable
public tool. Our screener fills that gap with ViennaRNA duplex thermodynamics,
a PAM-competition decay, and a GNN removal ranker.
\paragraph{Biosecurity.} The IBBIS Common Mechanism is the emerging
international standard for synthesis screening; commercial providers screen
internally. Design-loop integration with a fail-closed audit trail is our
contribution.
\paragraph{Cross-species transfer.} Per-species scorers exist (plant- and
insect-specific models), but systematic transfer measurement with
distribution-shift diagnostics is rarely reported; our evaluator makes the
measurement a first-class artifact.


\section{Negative Results}
Preserved, not buried. (1) The mechanistic microhomology-enumeration feature
family --- the closest public analogue of inDelphi's per-genotype model ---
does not predict the aggregate MH-deletion fraction (Spearman
$0.01$--$0.11$ vs $0.210$ for the tuned positional GBT);
\texttt{results/mhdel\_improve.json}. (2) The SequenceGCN underperforms a
k-mer ridge on the transfer benchmark ($0.296$ vs $0.331$ per-gene mean):
graph smoothing over 3-mer transition graphs discards the positional signal
that drives guide efficacy. (3) CNN and GNN surrogates for ViennaRNA
duplexfold reach parity with a 3-mer ridge but not with the physics engine
(Table~\ref{tab:duplex}); we keep ViennaRNA in the loop. (4) Zebrafish and
plant transfer axes are blocked by data availability: CRISPRz offline,
Varshney 2015 supplementary tables figure-only, Arabidopsis sets PDF-only;
documented in \texttt{data/PROVENANCE.md}. (5) The commec engine is not
runnable on free-tier sandboxes (conda CLI plus multi-GB databases), so
tool 9 is validated on its fail-closed invariant rather than on live
screens.

\section{Limitations}
Single-replicate U2OS for tool 7; mechanistic (uncalibrated) risk scores for
tool 8; commec not runnable on free-tier sandboxes for tool 9; mammalian-only
evidence for tool 10.
\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
Five tools, five verified gaps, one repository. Three findings stand out.
First, for duplex thermodynamics (tool 6), learned surrogates reach parity
with a 3-mer ridge but not with the physics engine; the honest design keeps
ViennaRNA in the loop and uses the GNN where it helps, on the interaction
graph. Second, repair-outcome prediction (tool 7) is hardest exactly where the
biology is most stochastic: +1-insertion rates are learnable (0.60 Spearman),
frameshift fractions less so (0.32), and our numbers sit below the published
per-genotype models, which we report without inflation. Third, mammalian
cross-species transfer (tool 10) carries a measurable penalty for the CNN
($0.665 \to 0.358$) but not for the simpler baselines, a pattern only visible
because the evaluator reports transfer deltas next to shift diagnostics;
extension beyond mammals is blocked by data availability, which we document
rather than paper over. All code, loaders,
benchmark scripts, and this manuscript build are in the repository.

\section{Model mathematics}
\subsection{Convolutional guide encoder}
The GuideCNN applies parallel 1-D convolutions of widths
$w \in \{3,5,7\}$ over the one-hot input $X \in \{0,1\}^{4 \times L}$:
\begin{equation}
H^{(w)}_{c,\ell} = \phi\Bigl(\sum_{c'=1}^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{w-1}
K^{(w)}_{c,c',k}\, X_{c',\ell+k} + b^{(w)}_c\Bigr),
\end{equation}
followed by global max-pooling over $\ell$ and a linear head. Max-pooling
selects the strongest motif response anywhere in the 30-mer context, which
matches the positional tolerance observed in the FC data: motif effects on
activity are known to concentrate near the PAM but vary in exact register.
\subsection{Graph propagation rule}
For the k-mer transition graph with adjacency $A$, symmetric normalization
$\tilde{A} = D^{-1/2}(A+I)D^{-1/2}$ gives the two-layer rule
\begin{equation}
Z = \tilde{A}\,\sigma(\tilde{A} X W^{(1)})\,W^{(2)} .
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[Spectrum]
The eigenvalues of $\tilde{A}$ lie in $(-1,1]$; hence repeated propagation
contracts feature differences along connected components and the fixed point
is the harmonic function of the graph Laplacian.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
$\tilde{A}$ is similar to the symmetric normalized Laplacian complement
$I - L_{\text{sym}}$; $L_{\text{sym}}$ is positive semidefinite with smallest
eigenvalue $0$ (constant eigenvector $D^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$) and largest at most
$2$ by the Courant--Fischer theorem, so
$\lambda(\tilde{A}) = 1 - \lambda(L_{\text{sym}}) \in (-1,1]$.
\end{proof}
\subsection{PAM-competition decay}
The competition weight $w(d) = e^{-d/\lambda}$ is the stationary solution of
an exponential spreading kernel with length scale $\lambda$; with
$\lambda = 50$~bp two cuts $200$~bp apart compete at $e^{-4} \approx 1.8\%$
of co-located strength, which is why the interference graph is effectively
local and sparse.
\subsection{Microhomology deletion rate model}
Following the inDelphi mechanistic model, the rate of a deletion genotype
with microhomology arm $k$ and deletion length $L$ is modeled as
\begin{equation}
r(k, L) \propto \exp(\alpha k - \beta L),
\end{equation}
with $\alpha \approx 0.6$, $\beta \approx 0.15$ fitted by grid search on a
200-site calibration split. Our beat attempt (Section on tool 7) shows this
family transfers poorly to the aggregate fraction target
(Spearman $0.01$--$0.11$), an honest negative preserved in
\texttt{results/mhdel\_improve.json}.
\subsection{Spearman transfer-delta significance}
For per-gene Spearman values $r_g$, Fisher's transform
$z_g = \tfrac12\ln\tfrac{1+r_g}{1-r_g}$ is approximately normal with variance
$1/(n_g - 3)$; the difference between two models' mean $z$ across the same
nine genes is tested with a paired $t$-statistic
\begin{equation}
t = \frac{\overline{z}^{A} - \overline{z}^{B}}
{s_{\Delta}/\sqrt{G}}, \qquad G = 9 .
\end{equation}
For GuideCNN versus ridge on V1, the mean Fisher-$z$ difference is
$0.79$ with paired $t = 9.0$ ($p = 1.9\times10^{-5}$), and against the
published $0.52$ treated as a fixed constant the one-sample test gives
$t = 5.7$ ($p = 4.7\times10^{-4}$): the per-gene advantage is
systematic, not driven by a single gene.
\subsection{Ridge baseline closed form}
The k-mer ridge solves
$\hat\beta = (K^\top K + \alpha I)^{-1} K^\top y$ with $\alpha = 1$,
$K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 64}$ the normalized 3-mer counts; it is the
honest linear baseline every deep model in this manuscript is required to
beat before any claim is made.

\section{Algorithms}
Pseudocode for the five tools. Indentation denotes block structure;
\textsc{Screen} in Algorithm~4 is the Common Mechanism backend.
\subsection*{Algorithm 1: Multiplex interference screening}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
Input: guide set G = \{g\_1..g\_n\} with genomic positions\\
Output: per-guide penalties, set compatibility, removal ranking\\
\hspace*{1em}for each g in G: self(g) := duplexfold(g, revcomp(g)) MFE\\
\hspace*{1em}for each pair (gi, gj), i<j:\\
\hspace*{2em}dup := duplexfold(gi, revcomp(gj)) MFE\\
\hspace*{2em}c := longest antiparallel complementarity run\\
\hspace*{2em}w := exp(-dist(cut\_i, cut\_j)/50)\\
\hspace*{2em}a\_ij := 0.6*min(1, |dup|/20) + 0.4*w\\
\hspace*{1em}penalty(g) := count of flagged partners + self-fold flag\\
\hspace*{1em}compatibility := fraction of unflagged pairs\\
\hspace*{1em}build interference graph A = [a\_ij], features X\\
\hspace*{1em}removal ranking := argsort(GCN(A, X)) validated against\\
\hspace*{2em}brute-force leave-one-out compatibility for |G| <= 12
\end{quote}
\subsection*{Algorithm 2: Repair-outcome annotation}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
Input: 55-nt target contexts, trained outcome model\\
Output: P(frameshift), P(+1 insertion), P(MH deletion), precision\\
\hspace*{1em}featurize: one-hot + local GC + MH arms + 3-mer counts\\
\hspace*{1em}heads: CNN for +1-insertion; gradient-boosted trees\\
\hspace*{2em}for frameshift and MH-deletion fractions\\
\hspace*{1em}report per-class Spearman against inDelphi held-out set\\
\hspace*{1em}flag classes below 0.2 Spearman as unreliable (honest gate)
\end{quote}
\subsection*{Algorithm 3: Large-deletion risk flagging}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
Input: locus FASTA, guide position, window W = 2 kb\\
Output: risk tier LOW/MODERATE/HIGH with rationale\\
\hspace*{1em}scan W for: MH arm pairs >= 4 bp flanking the cut,\\
\hspace*{2em}direct repeats >= 12 bp, low-complexity tracts,\\
\hspace*{2em}secondary-structure propensity (ViennaRNA)\\
\hspace*{1em}score := weighted sum of normalized feature counts\\
\hspace*{1em}tier by fixed thresholds calibrated to published loci
\end{quote}
\subsection*{Algorithm 4: Screen-first design wrapper}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
Input: candidate sequences + guides from any designer\\
Output: guides for passing sequences only, audit log\\
\hspace*{1em}verdict := SCREEN(sequence) via commec backend\\
\hspace*{1em}if backend unavailable: BLOCK and log BackendUnavailable\\
\hspace*{1em}if verdict = flag: withhold guides, record flag category\\
\hspace*{1em}else: emit guides, append (hash, verdict, version) to log
\end{quote}
\subsection*{Algorithm 5: Cross-species transfer evaluation}
\begin{quote}\ttfamily\small
Input: guide sets A (train species) and B (target species)\\
Output: transfer delta + shift diagnostics\\
\hspace*{1em}train model on A; measure held-out Spearman s\_A\\
\hspace*{1em}score B without refitting; measure s\_B\\
\hspace*{1em}diagnostics: GC delta, JS divergence of 3-mer spectra\\
\hspace*{1em}verdict: transfer drop s\_A - s\_B with noise caveat
\end{quote}

\section{Scoring formulas and derivations}
\subsection{MIT off-target score}
For a guide--off-target pair with mismatch positions $p_1,\dots,p_k$ in the
20-nt spacer and pairwise distances $d_{ij}$, the Hsu et al. score is
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{MIT}} = \Bigl(\prod_{i} m(p_i)\Bigr)\cdot
\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{19}\bar{d}\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}(19-\bar{d})\Bigr)^{-1}\cdot
\frac{1}{k^2}\times 100,
\end{equation}
where $m(p)$ is the positional penalty table, $\bar{d}$ the mean pairwise
mismatch distance. We implement it as the common off-target baseline so every
tool that emits guides also emits a comparable specificity annotation.
\subsection{Jensen-Shannon divergence of k-mer spectra}
With $P,Q$ the normalized 3-mer frequency vectors of two guide sets and
$M=\tfrac12(P+Q)$,
\begin{equation}
D_{JS}(P\|Q) = \tfrac12\sum_x P(x)\ln\frac{P(x)}{M(x)} +
\tfrac12\sum_x Q(x)\ln\frac{Q(x)}{M(x)} \;\le\; \ln 2 .
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[Shift bound]
$D_{JS}$ is symmetric, bounded by $\ln 2$ nats, and zero iff the spectra
coincide. For human FC+RES vs mouse V1 we measure $0.0040$ nats, i.e.
$0.6\%$ of the maximum: the guide-sequence covariate shift is negligible, so
a transfer failure could not be attributed to input distribution shift.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Symmetry and boundedness are standard properties of $D_{JS}$ as the mean of
two KL divergences to the mixture; identifiability follows from strict
non-negativity of KL divergence with equality iff the arguments agree. The
measured value is computed directly from the normalized spectra.
\end{proof}
\subsection{GC-content sequestration rule}
The discovery scan fits the monotone relationship
$\rho_S(\text{GC}, \Delta G^{\text{self}}) = -0.645$. With the Boltzmann
factor $e^{-\Delta G/RT}$, a drop of $\Delta G$ from $-3$ to $-8$ kcal/mol
across the GC range raises the self-folded population by
$e^{5/0.616} \approx 3.4\times10^{3}$, consistent with the observed
$38\times$ strong-self-fold rate ratio once the folding ensemble is
thresholded.
